Ancient Greece

City-States and the Polis

Ancient Greek civilization was never a single unified state. Instead it was
organized into independent city-states, called poleis, such as Athens,
Sparta, Corinth, and Thebes, each with its own government, laws, and army.
Rivalry and occasional cooperation between these city-states shaped most of
Greek history.

Democracy in Athens

Athens developed one of history's earliest democracies around 508 BC, under
the reformer Cleisthenes. Citizens participated directly in the Ecclesia,
the assembly, where any adult male citizen could speak and vote on laws and
policy. This system of direct participation was very different from the
representative democracies common today.

The Spartan Military System

Sparta built an entirely different kind of society, centered almost
completely on military training. Spartan boys entered a harsh state
training program called the agoge at around age seven, and remained
full-time soldiers for most of their adult lives.

The Peloponnesian War

The Peloponnesian War was fought between Athens and Sparta, along with
their respective allies, from 431 to 404 BC. It ended in a Spartan victory
and marked the beginning of a long decline in Athenian power and influence.

Legacy of Greek Philosophy

Classical Greece produced philosophers whose work still shapes Western
thought, most notably Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle. Plato founded a
school called the Academy in Athens around 387 BC, widely considered one
of the first institutions of higher learning in the Western world.
